#systematic-design 
### Reductionism
Reductionism is an approach to understanding systems by breaking them down to their individual parts and focussing on those single parts. While this can be very useful in some systems, where the system is focussed and orderly, with little side-effects, but doesn't work well when many individual parts all interact with each other in varied and messy ways, especially when that results in emergent behaviour.
### Holism
Holism is effectively the opposite of reductionism, it focusses on understanding the whole system, rather than focussing on the individual components. While this works well when the systems are highly interconnected with new, emergent behaviour, it can be very difficult to truly understand very complex systems, resulting overly broad or vague understandings.
### Connections
##### Interconnectedness
In every system, there will be interconnected smaller systems and components and interconnectedness is the 'amount' they are connected, and how closely
##### Boundaries
Defining what things are 'in' the system or 'out' of the system can be quite difficult. One example would be a school - yes the classrooms and teachers are part of the system, but are the pupils? Are their parents?
### Loops
Feedback loops are where a system's output is then fed back into the system, either reinforcing or dampening the output of said system. They are often the cause of emergent behaviour or, less positively, they are also the cause of many rapid system failures.
##### Positive Feedback Loops
An action a system takes produces an output that, when fed back into the system causes the action to become stronger over time, snowballing into exponential growth.
##### Negative Feedback Loops
An action a system takes produces an output that, when fed back into the system, causes the action to be dampened, or against the action, slowing down the system and bringing the state back to stability.
